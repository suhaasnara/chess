    Chapter 1

    Chess When I Was a Kid

    I started playing chess when I was four when my dad introduced it to me. My parents initially introduced it to my sister, but I ended up going to a tournament and loving it. I played a lot with my dad and remember the day I could finally beat him.

    For most of elementary school, I was one of the top-ranked players my age in the country. In third grade, I tied for 4th in the K-3 National Tournament.

    That looks great on paper. But it was a lot of pressure from a young age, and I started to explore other interests. By middle school, I was burning out and dived deeper into those interests. I put that energy into STEM and quiz competitions instead: Science Bowl, Ocean Bowl, Science Olympiad, and research. By high school I had more or less retired from serious chess.

    Chapter 2

    The Comeback

    I came back during COVID, after my senior year of high school. The Queen's Gambit and the streaming boom had everyone talking about chess, and friends started asking me to play. I started on Lichess, joined my college chess club, and during some time off after my sophomore year I rediscovered opening study through Hanging Pawns. My early repertoire was the Trompowsky, Exchange lines against d5 structures, the Tarrasch, and the Hyper-Accelerated Dragon.

    The biggest factor in my comeback was my hometown chess community. My friends participated in and ran the Gryphon Games, a Saturday tournament every other week, and I played in them constantly. I won a lot of them, usually while playing down. That's also where my style settled into what it is today: calm and controlled. I'll admit I also picked up a serious Chessable course habit along the way.

    Back Home

    Top of the State

    I became one of the top players in my state, went on a roughly 37-game undefeated streak over the board, and trained online with other serious chess improvers. My rating peaked at 2146.

    So why didn't my rating climb faster?
    • Few strong events. My state didn't have as many strong or regular tournaments, and lacking K40 (a higher K-factor that lets young players' ratings rise and fall faster) hurt.
    • Playing down. Winning against lower-rated players didn't gain a lot of points.
    • Avoiding tactics. I dodged tactics training until the Woodpecker Method showed me it could be fun.
    • Life. School and other priorities came first for me.
    Chapter 3

    The Final Push to NM

    Back at university, chess slid down my priority list. My decline started at the Penn Fall Swiss that October, and a rough winter break and a bad Liberty Bell Open dropped me below 2100, after I had peaked at 2146. That was my low point, and it forced me to rethink how I trained.

    What I changed

    • Finished the Woodpecker Method. Tactics finally stopped being something I avoided.
    • Narrowed my repertoire. I dropped the Catalan for the Nimzo and the QGD Exchange, and moved to a simpler, system-based repertoire as Black.
    • Focused on one or two things at a time instead of trying to fix everything at once.
    • Worked through some of Perfect Your Chess and played training games with lots of review.
    • Played for wins, not perfect games, and got serious about time management.
    Summer

    Shaking Off the Rust

    A local summer tournament started rusty and finished with strong wins, taking me from 2084 back into the 2100s.

    Philly Open

    My Best Event

    A draw, then three straight wins (two of them near-perfect games against 2000+ players), then a final-round draw with GM Maxim Dlugy as Black.

    Denver

    Invitational Wins

    I beat a 2000-rated player and FM Gunnar Andersen, Colorado's current strongest player, at an invitational event.

    2203

    National Master

    I left for Penn at 2187 and played regular local quads. Funnily enough, I took an early draw as Black in a position that was -5 in my favor, and got stuck at 2199.29. Finally I won a first-round game against an 1890 at the Penn Fall Swiss, withdrew, and finished at 2203. In another twist of fate, the Penn Fall Swiss is where my rating decline had started. It all came full circle.

    I'm grateful for every training partner, opponent, coach, and friend who made it worth it. NM really is the friends we made along the way.

    Chess Improvement

    What worked for me, what didn't, and where I'd point you instead.

    Overview

    Start Here

    I came back to chess wanting to reach NM for fun and for pride. This guide is part reflection and part giving back. It lays out the structure and methods I used. The ideas are mostly my own unless I cite someone.

    If you take one thing away: do what makes chess fun for you. A rating goal shouldn't be an end in itself. I don't regret the training quirks that made the game fun for me, even the ones that weren't "optimal."

    Who should you trust?

    There is no chess god and no scripture. Improvement advice works more like academia: people test ideas and share what they found. Try what's commonly recommended, keep what works for you, and drop what doesn't. That includes everything on this site.

    It's also worth spending ten minutes on your Chess Player Profile (just below) before you pick a plan.

    Know Thyself

    Your Chess Player Profile

    Some say the whole of life is experiencing yourself. It's useful to understand who you are and what you want to get out of chess.

    Before diving into a training plan, consider these questions honestly:

    Who Are You?

    Your occupation, time commitment, life stage, and priorities all shape how much you can realistically invest in chess improvement.

    What Is Chess to You?

    Hobby? Obsession? Time-killer? What do you currently get out of chess, and what do you want to get out of it?

    What Type of Player Are You?

    Sharp and attacking, or calm and positional? Booked up or intuitive? Lots of online blitz, or OTB classical only? Know thyself.

    What Drives You?

    Identify what keeps you motivated, what causes burnout, and where chess sits in the hierarchy of your life. This shapes everything.

    My example

    What I get out of chess is fun, goodness, truth, and beauty. My favorite piece is the pawn because of how essential but underrated they are. Seemingly simple things like pawn structure and breaks can shape an entire game.

    The Big Picture

    The Areas of Chess Training

    Chess training takes many forms. These are the core areas I've found, and each one has more specific skills to work on as you progress. There's a lot to improve, which is both daunting and exciting: there's always something new to learn.

    The good news is that you rarely get weaker in the short term (one to six months). You just get rusty or less sharp. Thanks to muscle memory, regaining your form is much easier than building it the first time.

    My daily routine
    1. 1A few tacticsI recommend at least 5 Lichess puzzles a day to stay sharp.
    2. 2A rapid game
    3. 3A review of that game
    If you only do one thing, do this.

    Openings

    Setting a repertoire (White, Black, sidelines), learning the ideas and lines, then practice games and review.

    Middlegames

    Strategy and a toolkit of positional and tactical ideas. Calculation: tactics, combinations, and visualization. Then practice positions and puzzles.

    Endgames

    Both practical and theoretical endgames, plus practice games and review.

    Psychology

    Mindset, your philosophy of the game, and mindfulness at the board.

    Decision Making

    Frameworks for choosing a move, and scenario analysis when there's no clear answer.

    Resources

    Knowing how to use an engine, opening files, books, videos, and homework. See Resources.

    How to Train

    What Should I Do at My Level?

    I'm not going to reinvent the wheel here. For a level-by-level training plan, I point people to ChessDojo. It's a training program covering ratings from 0 to 2500, with free and paid tiers, founded by GM Jesse Kraai, IM Kostya Kavutskiy, and IM David Pruess. Its Discord is very active, with training games and book clubs.

    What I like about it is the focus on fundamentals:

    • Play lots of games. Serious games are where improvement starts.
    • Review without an engine first. Work out what happened yourself before checking.
    • Analyze together. Reviewing games with other people catches what you'd miss alone.
    • Cover all the bases. Openings, middlegames, endgames, tactics, and calculation all get time.
    Learn from my mistake: I went too far in one direction, burned out, and then came up short in my weakest areas, endgames and tactics. A balanced plan would have saved me a lot of time.
    My Favorite Training Method

    How I Review Games

    After any longer game (10+ minutes), add it to a Lichess study. I keep all my training games together in one study so I can see my progress and spot patterns over time. I also add each game to my opening file for that line (more on that under Openings).

    To track results over time, I use a self-analysis spreadsheet for openings and for tournament and training games. Feel free to make a copy.

    1. 1

      Annotate first, without an engine

      Write down your thoughts move by move. Keep notes brief and to the point, like "expected 7...Ng4 but he went 7...h5."

      Add the key variations you considered and what you thought of them.

    2. 2

      Then check with the engine

      Request a computer analysis on Lichess to see your accuracy and blunder count. It's the Lichess version of Chess.com's Game Review.

      Compare it with your notes. Where the engine disagreed, write down what it found and why you missed it. For example: "Engine found Nxe5! but I thought there wasn't enough compensation."

    3. 3

      Don't over-rely on the engine

      It's a fact checker. It can give useful insight, but don't take its first word for granted. If a line feels funky, play it further until it shows something concrete.

      Ignore tiny differences. A gap of 0.2 or less usually isn't worth your time.

    Tournament games

    Follow the same process, but go into more detail. You'll have calculated many more lines and had many more thoughts during a tournament game, so get them down. Don't be tempted to open the engine before you annotate. Gukesh famously trained without engines for years.

    Keep a tournament journal

    During downtime between rounds, write about your mental state, your results, how you feel about your play, your lunch, the nice people you met, whatever comes to mind. It helps in three ways:

    • It keeps things impersonal. You see your results more objectively, which reduces tilt.
    • It gives you real downtime instead of worrying about the next game.
    • It's fascinating to look back on. Sometimes the engine says you won a smooth game while your journal says you were sweating at the board, unsure your attack would work.
    The First Moves

    Opening Study

    Before picking openings, it helps to know what kind of positions you enjoy. Here's how some common openings map out, plus the vocabulary I use:

    The full vocabulary
    ProfileWhat it means
    ClosedLocked pawn chains and slow maneuvering
    Semi-openOne side's file opens early, so the game is unbalanced from the start
    OpenEarly pawn trades, open lines, and active pieces
    SharpConcrete, double-edged, and tactical
    PositionalStructure and long-term plans matter more than forcing lines
    HypermodernControl the center from a distance, then strike at it
    ClassicalOccupy the center directly with pawns
    SystemThe same setup against almost anything
    GambitMaterial for initiative
    ReversedA known Black opening played as White with an extra tempo
    SymmetricalBoth sides mirror each other's setup

    For what it's worth, I dislike symmetrical positions and gravitate toward classical openings, systems, and positional lines.

    Learning a new opening

    • Test the waters. Watch videos, read something short, browse the Lichess masters database, or try a free Chessable "Short & Sweet" course. Check whether it gets you positions you actually want to play.
    • Learn the main ideas. A coach can explain them quickly. My students ask me in a lesson, and I send files to study on their own time.
    • Then commit. Give an opening a fair shot before switching, and write down why you like or dislike it.
    • Don't min-max. You'll play better in positions you enjoy than in the "objectively best" line.

    Keep opening files

    After each longer game, add it to your opening file for that line, not just your training-games study. That way your comments and the common ideas sit right next to your real games. If I play a 10-minute game in the 7.g4 Semi-Slav, it goes in both places. Then I check where I went wrong and review the relevant lines, so either they don't out-prep me again or I go in more confident next time.

    The week before a tournament

    Brush up on your lines. If you have a Chessable course, run through its quick review for each system you play. It's one of the best ways to feel confident in your repertoire, because no other area improves as quickly in a week. Still keep up tactics and other training so you stay sharp.

    Going Deeper

    Prep Work and Process

    Below NM, I'd rely on courses for your actual lines. Building your own prep can eat a lot of time without making you play better.

    • Tools. ChessBase is the gold standard, but I reached NM using Lichess Studies. They work like ChessBase, minus some useful features, but are completely free, shareable, and editable in real time. HIARCS is a good option on Mac, and chessdb is useful for engine evaluations (use it with care).
    • Process. Combine a strong engine, the masters database, and your own brain.

    Picking a prep style

    I classify prep using a 2×2 matrix: ambitious or solid, mainline or offbeat. Choose the quadrant that fits the situation, for example a game you must win.

    AmbitiousSolid

    Ambitious + Mainline

    Live and die by the sword, Fischer and Kasparov style.

    Ambitious + Offbeat

    Highest risk and reward. This is the modern engine-prep approach, like Nxc6!? in the Accelerated Dragon.

    Solid + Mainline

    Get an intuitive and playable game, like Seirawan's English.

    Solid + Offbeat

    Get a calm, imbalanced position and outplay them later. This is the typical club player's profile, and it described my old Hyper-Accelerated Dragon.

    Chess Communities

    Your chess community is simply the people you do chess with, and it's the single most important factor in my own comeback. Find one through a Google search, a local club, tournaments, or pub chess.

    Online, Free

    Discord servers: several are full of 2000+ OTB players, many of them titled. Also ChessDojo, Chess.com, and Lichess.

    Further Reading

    Resources

    Where to play and train

    • Lichess: completely free. Unlimited puzzles with lots of themes if you get bored of the usual ones, plus studies for building your own database and browsing free public lessons. Public studies are especially good for drilling specific positions, like rook endgames.
    • Chess.com: some features need a paid membership, which I don't think is necessary. If you already pay, its lessons from world-class coaches cover lots of topics and are usually high quality.
    • Listudy: more free tactics training.
    • Chessable: digital courses from world-class authors that make it easy to train and follow the comments. Full courses cost money, but up to five "Short & Sweet" opening courses are free per account. They're my top pick for figuring out which openings you like.

    Chess media

    Watch a wide variety of chess content that interests you, some for fun and some for serious study. I'm a huge fan of the late Daniel Naroditsky (1995-2025). He was a wonderful teacher who shared his love of the game, and his speedruns remain one of the best windows into how a top GM thinks. The US Chess School lectures are advanced but very good. I also enjoy recaps of top tournaments and following the pro scene, and there's plenty of short-form content if that's more your speed.

    The Foundation

    Core Principles

    Understand, Don't Memorize

    Improvement comes from grasping the why behind each move. I teach principles so you can reason through positions you've never seen before.

    Learn from Every Game

    Losses are your greatest teachers. If you were only 12 losses away from your goal, you'd be excited to lose. Analyze your own games without an engine first. This builds the critical thinking that creates improvement.

    Endgames First

    Most players neglect endgames. As a coach, I prioritize them because they reveal the true nature of chess: piece activity, king power, and precision. These are fundamentals that flow into every phase.

    Consistent, Deliberate Practice

    An hour of focused study beats five hours of casual play. I help students build routines that make improvement sustainable over the long run.

    Tactics, Tactics, Tactics

    Chess is calculation. No matter your level, a daily tactics habit is the highest-ROI training activity there is.

    Do What Makes Chess Fun

    This is the one takeaway I want for everyone. A rating goal is not an end in itself. You won't enjoy your goal if you hate the process.
